A briefing for the Atlantic Data Institute

How Trump wins online, and what other politicians can take from it

I scored 89,324 of his posts on Twitter and Truth Social against his own normal at the time, to find what his base rewards, what it ignores and which lessons still hold in 2026.

Armaan Khan, for Knox. 26 November 2026.

The short version

Picking fights pays

Posts that give an opponent a nickname or attack the media do 16 to 25% better than his posts without them, on both platforms and across ten years. Praise is his weakest tone.

His own words beat everything else

On Truth Social, posts written in his own words score 1.23 times his normal. Posts that are only an image or video score 0.89, and posts that are only a link score 0.74.

Every extra post dilutes the rest

On days he posts one to three times, each post scores 1.64 times his normal. On days with more than 30 posts, each one scores 0.80.

His favourite topic is wearing out

Elections make up a quarter of his Truth Social posts in his own words, and are his weakest topic. Election posts have scored lower every year since 2023, while everything else has held steady.

Truth Social holds the base, but it didn't win 2024

His audience there is a ninth of his old Twitter following, though each follower engages more. The groups that moved towards him in 2024, such as young men and first-time voters, were the ones his campaign chased on podcasts and creator channels.

Seventeen years in one chart

He went from TV personality (2009 to 2014) to candidate (2015 to 2016) and president (2017 to 2020) on Twitter. After the ban that followed 6 January 2021, he launched his own platform, Truth Social, in 2022, and now governs from it. He now posts more than he did in his first term: about 16 posts of his own a day, up from 11.

How each post is scored

A like in 2015 isn't worth the same as a like in 2025. His audience grew, Twitter grew, and Truth Social is far smaller than Twitter ever was. So I compared each post with his typical post that month on the same platform. A score of 1 means a post got his usual number of likes for that month, 2 means double and 0.5 means half.

That gives 49,381 scored posts: 23,580 tweets from his campaign launch in July 2015 to January 2021, and 25,801 Truth Social posts from 2022 to April 2026. Reposts of other people's posts aren't scored.

Why does he do so well on social media?

Three habits run through ten years of posts. He picks fights, he writes the way he talks, and he never lets the news cycle move on without him.

He picks fights

Nicknames for opponents, attacks on the media and mentions of the other side all lift a post, on Twitter and Truth Social alike. He isn't unusual here. A study of 2.7 million posts by US news outlets and members of Congress found that each word about the political out-group raised the odds of a post being shared by 67%.

He writes the way he talks

Capitals and exclamation marks help too, and anything that isn't his own words does worse. On Truth Social, an image or video on its own scores 0.89 and a bare link 0.74, against 1.23 for a post in his own words. Links do badly on both platforms. A link asks people to leave, and his followers are there to hear from him.

He never lets the news cycle move on

The more he posts in a day, the less each post gets. On Truth Social, each post scores 1.64 on his quietest days and 0.80 on days with more than 30 posts. The volume still wins him attention overall, because there is always a new post to react to, but each message is weaker for it.

Timing matters less than volume. On Truth Social, his posts between 7 and 9am (US Eastern) do best and his late-morning posts do worst. As president on Twitter, 8 to 11am was his best window.

These effects hold up when tested together. A statistical model of his Truth Social posts, which also allows for topic, hour and how much he posted that day, finds that a link costs a post 21% of its score. Nicknames add 13%, exclamation marks 13%, media attacks 9% and heavy capitals 7%. Each doubling of his posts in a day lowers each one's score by about 16%.

What this means

Conflict and a personal voice travel further than polish. The habit others shouldn't copy is the volume. A sitting president stays the story whatever he posts. Anyone else gets more from fewer, sharper posts.

Which posts do best and worst with his base?

His base rewards strength and grievance aimed at a clear target. It has tired of election talk, and it barely reacts to praise.

Truth Social is the closest thing in this data to a direct line to his base. In Pew's 2022 study, two-thirds of people who got news on alternative platforms like it were Republicans, and half of its most prominent accounts were pro-Trump or right-leaning.

What works

Foreign policy (1.42), immigration (1.35) and his legal battles (1.33). Threats are his strongest tone at 1.68, followed by policy announcements such as new tariffs (1.41), denials (1.39), and boasts and accusations (both 1.35).

What flops

Elections, a quarter of his posts in his own words, score 1.09. Election posts scored 1.24 in 2023 and 0.74 in early 2026, while his other posts stayed between 1.22 and 1.40. Praise, his second most common tone, scores 1.02, barely above his normal. Treat that one with some care: in my hand check, 5 of 13 posts labelled Praise were really him praising himself or asking people to act.

What this means

His two weakest areas are things he does a lot, so habit is costing him reach. Any politician can run the same check on their own feed: score each topic against their own normal every month, and drop a message once it stops landing.

What can other politicians learn about the groups in his coalition?

His own feed holds his base together. The groups that swung to him in 2024 were reached somewhere else.

The Knox Index measures how far a politician reaches beyond the political bubble, and that is the right test here. Truth Social is a loyal, closed room: a ninth of his old Twitter audience, with more engagement from each follower. The groups that moved most in 2024 were the ones his campaign went looking for on podcasts, sport channels and creator shows.

What this means

Keep the two jobs apart. Conflict-heavy content energises people who already agree with you. Reaching anyone else takes different formats, voices and places, usually outside politics. Copying his base strategy without his base gets you the fights without the loyalty.

How does he compare with other senior US politicians?

Attacks lift engagement for everyone. The standout new success of 2025 did almost the opposite.

The study behind the 67% figure looked at members of Congress too: each word about the other side lifted shares by 65 to 180%. Since 2024, other politicians have tried his methods, with mixed results.

Newsom shows that copying his style gets attention quickly, mostly from people who already oppose Trump. Mamdani shows the other route: a positive, local, video-first voice that reached people who don't usually follow politics. That is the bridge Trump's own feed doesn't build.

What this means

His tactics can be borrowed. His position can't. The politicians who gained most in 2025 brought his energy to a voice of their own.

2016 or 2026: what still works and what has changed

Still works

  • Conflict with a named target
  • A recognisable personal voice
  • Posts written for the platform instead of links away from it
  • Fewer, sharper posts, for anyone who isn't the president

Has changed

  • Video comes first. One in five US adults now regularly get news on TikTok, up from 3% in 2020, and so do 39% of under-30s.
  • X reaches far fewer Americans than YouTube (84% of adults) or Facebook (71%).
  • AI-made images are normal now, even from official accounts.
  • Podcasts and creators reached the swing voters of 2024.

A playbook for 2026

  1. Have a target

    Contrast drives sharing. Aim it at an opponent or an issue, and never at the voters you want.

  2. Sound like a person

    Write in your own voice and post directly on the platform. Links and polished graphics both cost reach.

  3. Ration your posts

    Each extra post dilutes the rest. Post when your audience is online, which is rarely when the office opens.

  4. Watch for fatigue

    Score your own posts by topic every month, and retire messages that have stopped working.

  5. Split base and bridge

    Use your own channels for supporters. Reach everyone else through creators, podcasts and short video.

The tool I built: a post scorer for any politician

Everything above comes from a small Python tool I built so the same analysis can run on anyone's posts. It takes a spreadsheet with a date, the text and the like counts for each post, and works in four steps. The numbers below are from this run.

  1. Clean

    89,325 rows in. I dropped 1 duplicate, rebuilt the broken IDs, separated 16,957 reposts and converted every time to US Eastern.

  2. Tag

    Style tags by fixed rules. Topics from a text model trained and tested on 14,161 labelled posts, right 73% of the time on posts it hadn't seen.

  3. Score

    Each post against his median that month on the same platform. 49,381 posts scored.

  4. Scorecard

    Four measures modelled on the Knox Index, plus my own overall average.

The overall score is my own simple average of the four measures, because Knox's own formula isn't public. Followers aren't in the data, so they come from news reports: 88.7 million on Twitter when he was banned, and more than 10 million on Truth Social in mid-2025. With the August 2026 figure of about 13 million, his engagement rate would be 0.10%, still above his Twitter rate.

The same scorecard could run on any leader's archive and sit alongside the Knox Index World Leaders view. The code, the notebook and instructions are in the project on GitHub.

How I did it

The data was 56,571 tweets (May 2009 to January 2021) and 32,754 Truth Social posts (February 2022 to April 2026), supplied by Knox for this brief. I cleaned and analysed it in Python (pandas and scikit-learn) with SQL queries in DuckDB, and built the charts with Chart.js.

Checking the labels by hand

I checked a random sample of 80 posts by hand: 50 Truth Social posts with the labels that came in the file, and 30 tweets with the topic my model gave them. Answers I wasn't sure about are left out.

The supplied topic labels were right for 43 of 47 posts, and for all 37 on the six topics this report uses. The supplied tone labels were right for 41 of 48, but Praise was the weakest: 5 of 13 posts labelled Praise were really something else, mostly him praising himself. My model's topics for tweets were right for 25 of 30, with Media the weakest. One person did this check, so treat the percentages as rough. It shows the topic findings can be trusted and the praise finding needs care.

Where I used AI

I used Claude, an AI assistant, to help write the Python and SQL, build the charts and this page, and speed up the outside research. I set the approach and made the decisions: how to score posts, what to drop, where scoring starts and which findings matter. I checked 80 of the supplied labels by hand and reviewed all the code and output. Those checks caught mistakes in AI drafts, including a claim that he posts more than ever (he posted more in 2013) and a best posting time based on only 21 posts.
