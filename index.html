A briefing for the Atlantic Data Institute

How Trump wins online, and what other politicians can take from it

I scored 49,381 of his posts on Twitter and Truth Social, out of 89,324 in the files, against his own normal at the time, to find what his base rewards, what it ignores and which lessons still hold in 2026.

Armaan Khan, for Knox. 26 November 2026.

The short version

Picking fights pays

Posts that give an opponent a nickname or attack the media do 16 to 25% better than his posts without them, on both platforms and across ten years. Praise is his weakest tone.

His own words beat everything else

On Truth Social, posts written in his own words score 1.23 times his normal. Posts that are only an image or video score 0.89, and posts that are only a link score 0.74.

Each extra post gets less

On days he posts one to three times, each post scores 1.64 times his normal. On days with more than 30 posts, each one scores 0.80, although the day as a whole gets more attention.

His favourite topic is wearing out

Elections make up a quarter of his Truth Social posts in his own words, and are his weakest topic, as they were in most years on Twitter. On Truth Social the gap has widened every year since 2023.

Truth Social holds the base, but it didn't win 2024

He still has more than 100 million followers on X but posts there far less, because Truth Social gets his posts first. Truth Social reaches about 10 to 13 million people, who engage about as much per follower as his Twitter followers did. The groups that moved towards him in 2024, such as young men and first-time voters, were the ones his campaign targeted on podcasts and creator channels.

Seventeen years in one chart

He went from TV personality (2009 to 2014) to candidate (2015 to 2016) and president (2017 to 2020) on Twitter. After the ban that followed 6 January 2021, he launched his own platform, Truth Social, in 2022, and now governs from it. He now posts more than he did in his first term: about 16 posts of his own a day, up from 11.

How each post is scored

A like in 2015 isn't worth the same as a like in 2025. His audience grew, Twitter grew, and Truth Social is far smaller than Twitter ever was. So I compared each post with his typical post that month on the same platform. A score of 1 means a post got his usual number of likes for that month, 2 means double and 0.5 means half. His usual includes image and link posts, so a typical text post scores about 1.2. Read the topic and tone charts against that.

That gives 49,381 scored posts: 23,580 tweets from his campaign launch in July 2015 to January 2021, and 25,801 Truth Social posts from 2022 to April 2026. Reposts of other people's posts aren't scored. When I say posts in his own words, I mean text posts, which sometimes quote other people or a headline.

Why does he do so well on social media?

Three habits run through ten years of posts. He picks fights, he writes the way he talks, and he never lets the news cycle move on without him.

He picks fights

Nicknames for opponents, attacks on the media and mentions of the other side all lift a post, on Twitter and Truth Social alike. He isn't unusual here. A study of 2.7 million posts by US news outlets and members of Congress found that each word about the political out-group raised the odds of a post being shared by 67%.

He writes the way he talks

Capitals and exclamation marks help too, and anything that isn't his own words does worse. On Truth Social, an image or video on its own scores 0.89 and a bare link 0.74, against 1.23 for a post in his own words. Links do badly on both platforms. A link asks people to leave, and his followers are there to hear from him. Image and video posts also do worse on his own text-first platform, even though video is the main format on TikTok and YouTube.

He never lets the news cycle move on

The more he posts in a day, the less each post gets. On Truth Social, each post scores 1.64 on his quietest days and 0.80 on days with more than 30 posts. The volume still wins him attention overall, because there is always a new post to react to, but each message is weaker for it.

Timing matters less than volume, and these differences shrink once topic and volume are taken into account. On Truth Social, his posts between 7 and 9am (US Eastern) do best and his late-morning posts do worst. As president on Twitter, 8 to 11am was his best window.

These effects hold up when tested together. A statistical model of his Truth Social posts, which also allows for topic, hour and how much he posted that day, finds that a link costs a post 21% of its score. Nicknames add 13%, exclamation marks 13%, media attacks 9% and heavy capitals 7%. Each doubling of his posts in a day lowers each one's score by about 16%.

What this means

Having a target and writing in your own voice both help, on both platforms. Volume is a trade-off: posting a lot keeps him in the news, but each post gets less. I've only tested this on Trump, and most politicians don't get a president's attention, so fewer posts that each land may suit them better.

Which posts do best and worst with his base?

His base rewards strength and grievance aimed at a clear target. Election posts do worst, and praise barely moves it.

Truth Social is the closest thing in this data to a direct line to his base. In Pew's 2022 study, two-thirds of people who got news on alternative platforms like it were Republicans, and half of its most prominent accounts were pro-Trump or right-leaning.

What works

Foreign policy (1.42), immigration (1.35) and his legal battles (1.33). Threats are his strongest tone at 1.68, followed by policy announcements such as new tariffs (1.41), denials (1.39), and boasts and accusations (both 1.35).

What flops

Elections, a quarter of his posts in his own words, score 1.09. Election tweets trailed his other tweets in most years on Twitter too, and on Truth Social the gap has grown: election posts scored 1.24 in 2023 and 0.74 in early 2026, while his other posts stayed between 1.22 and 1.40. Fatigue is one explanation. Another is that the content changed after 2024, from campaigning to claims about 2020. Praise, his second most common tone, scores 1.02, about 17% below a typical text post. Treat that one with some care: in my hand check, 5 of 13 posts labelled Praise were really him praising himself or asking people to act.

What this means

His two weakest areas are things he does a lot, so habit is costing him reach. Any politician can run the same check on their own feed: score each topic against their own normal every month, and drop a message once it stops landing.

What can other politicians learn about the groups in his coalition?

His own feed holds his base together. The groups that swung to him in 2024 were reached somewhere else.

The useful test here is how far a politician reaches beyond people who already follow politics. Truth Social is a loyal, closed room of about 10 to 13 million followers, a fraction of his 100 million-plus on X. The groups that moved most in 2024 were the ones his campaign targeted on podcasts, sport channels and creator shows.

What this means

Keep the two jobs apart. Conflict-heavy content energises people who already agree with you. Reaching anyone else takes different formats, voices and places, usually outside politics.

How does he compare with other senior US politicians?

Attacks lift engagement for everyone. The standout new success of 2025 did almost the opposite.

For members of Congress alone, the effect in that study was larger: each word about the other side lifted shares by 65 to 180%. Since 2024, other politicians have tried his methods, with mixed results.

Newsom shows that copying his style gets attention quickly, mostly from people who already oppose Trump. Mamdani shows the other route: a positive, local, video-first voice that reached people who don't usually follow politics. Trump's own feed doesn't reach those people in the same way.

What this means

Others can borrow his tactics, but not his position as president. The politicians who gained most in 2025 brought his energy to a voice of their own.

2016 or 2026: what still works and what has changed

Still works

  • Conflict with a named target
  • A recognisable personal voice
  • Posts written for the platform instead of links away from it
  • Fewer posts, if you want each one to land

Has changed

  • Video comes first. One in five US adults now regularly get news on TikTok, up from 3% in 2020, and so do 43% of under-30s.
  • He's back on X, with more than 100 million followers, but posts there far less, because Truth Social gets his posts first.
  • AI-made images are normal now, even from official accounts.
  • Podcasts and creators reached the swing voters of 2024.

A playbook for 2026

  1. Have a target

    Contrast drives sharing. Aim it at an opponent or an issue, and never at the voters you want.

  2. Sound like a person

    Write in your own voice and post directly on the platform. Links and image-only posts both did worse for him.

  3. Decide on volume

    Each extra post gets less, even if the day's total grows. Decide which matters more to you.

  4. Watch for fatigue

    Score your own posts by topic every month, and retire messages that have stopped working.

  5. Split base and bridge

    Use your own channels for supporters. Reach everyone else through creators, podcasts and short video.

The tool I built: a post scorer for any politician

I did the analysis in the notebook, then packaged its core steps into a small Python tool so the same scoring can run on anyone's posts. The four steps below are the analysis, with this run's numbers. The tool runs the cleaning, style tags, scoring and scorecard on any export with a date, the text and like counts. Topic labelling stays in the notebook, because it needs labelled posts to learn from.

  1. Clean

    89,325 rows in. I dropped 1 duplicate, rebuilt the broken IDs, separated 16,957 reposts and converted every time to US Eastern.

  2. Tag

    Style tags by fixed rules. Topics from a text model trained and tested on 14,161 labelled posts, right 73% of the time on posts it hadn't seen.

  3. Score

    Each post against his median that month on the same platform. 49,381 posts scored.

  4. Scorecard

    Four measures modelled on the Knox Index, plus my own overall average.

The figures behind the scorecard
MeasureTwitter 2017 to 2020Truth Social 2025 to 2026

The measures are named after the Knox Index, but the maths is mine: engagement here is median likes per post as a share of followers, and the overall score is a simple average, because Knox's formula isn't public. Followers aren't in the data, so I used the middle of the start and end figures from news reports. On Twitter that's about 20 million when he took office and 88.7 million at the ban. On Truth Social it's more than 10 million in mid-2025 and about 13 million by August 2026. On that basis, engagement per follower is about the same on both: 0.13% and 0.12%.

Scored against a fixed benchmark instead of each other, the same scorecard could compare any leader's archive. The code, the notebook and instructions are in the project on GitHub.

How I did it

The data was 56,571 tweets (May 2009 to January 2021) and 32,754 Truth Social posts (February 2022 to April 2026), supplied by Knox for this brief. I cleaned and analysed it in Python (pandas and scikit-learn) with SQL queries in DuckDB, and built the charts with Chart.js.

Checking the labels by hand

I checked a random sample of 80 posts by hand: 50 Truth Social posts with the labels that came in the file, and 30 tweets with the topic my model gave them. Answers I wasn't sure about are left out.

The supplied topic labels were right for 43 of 47 posts, and for all 37 on the six topics this report uses. The supplied tone labels were right for 41 of 48, but Praise was the weakest: 5 of 13 posts labelled Praise were really something else, mostly him praising himself. My model's topics for tweets were right for 25 of 30, with Media the weakest. One person did this check, so treat the percentages as rough. It shows the topic findings can be trusted and the praise finding needs care.

Where I used AI

I used AI the way I would at work: to speed up the repetitive, time-consuming parts, with me setting the direction and checking the results. I broke the task into stages and set the requirements for each one, such as plain pandas and SQL I could explain line by line, a light and readable page, and UK English. Claude then wrote most of the code, the charts and this page, and sped up the outside research. I made the decisions: how to score posts, what to drop, where scoring starts, and which findings matter for 2026.

I questioned and checked the work as it went. I asked for an outlier check like the one from my university coursework, and it found that early Twitter likes had been counted years later. I checked 80 of the supplied labels by hand, which is why the praise finding carries a warning. My checks also caught mistakes in AI drafts, such as a claim that he posts more than ever (he posted more in 2013) and a best posting time based on only 21 posts. Finally, I had a second AI review the finished report against the brief and fixed what it found, including how I compared followers across the two platforms. I used Grammarly to check spelling and grammar.

The full method note, the notebook and the tool's code are in the project on GitHub.
